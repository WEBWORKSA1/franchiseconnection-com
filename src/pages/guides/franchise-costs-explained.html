Franchise costs come in three layers: what you pay to get the right to operate, what you spend to open, and what you pay every month for as long as you own the business. Then there are the costs of leaving. The Franchise Disclosure Document must disclose all of them — Items 5, 6 and 7 cover most — but they are spread across the document, and the totals are rarely presented in one place.

Layer 1: the right to operate

Initial franchise fee (Item 5). Typically $20,000 to $60,000 for a single unit, sometimes lower for home-based concepts and higher for hotels or large-format brands. Multi-unit development agreements often discount the fee for second and later units but require a deposit up front. Veterans and conversion franchisees sometimes receive discounts. Ask whether any part is refundable if you do not open.

Layer 3: the monthly bill (Item 6)

  • Royalty. Usually a percentage of gross sales — commonly 4–8% — paid weekly or monthly. Some systems charge a flat fee or a tiered rate that falls as sales rise. Royalties are paid on revenue, not profit, so they are due even when you lose money.
  • Brand or ad fund. Often 1–4% of gross sales for national or regional marketing. Item 11 explains how the fund is governed and whether franchisees see accounts.
  • Local marketing requirement. A minimum amount you must spend in your own market, separate from the fund.
  • Technology fee. Monthly charges for POS, scheduling, CRM, websites and apps.
  • Required purchases. Products from the franchisor or approved suppliers, sometimes with rebates paid to the franchisor (Item 8).
  • Insurance. Minimum coverage levels set by the franchisor.
  • Training and conference fees. Annual conventions, new-manager training, refresher courses.
  • Remodel requirements. Periodic refreshes of the premises to current brand standards.

Layer 4: changing or leaving

  • Renewal fee at the end of the term, plus any required upgrades (Item 17).
  • Transfer fee when you sell, and the buyer must be approved and trained.
  • Right of first refusal letting the franchisor match a buyer's offer.
  • Termination costs: lost royalties damages in some agreements, de-identification of premises and equipment.
  • Non-compete restrictions that limit using your skills in the same industry for a period after leaving.

A worked example

Consider a unit with $700,000 in annual sales, a 6% royalty, a 2% brand fund and a $350 monthly technology fee. Ongoing franchise fees come to $60,200 a year. Over a ten-year term with 3% annual sales growth, that adds up to roughly $684,000 before local marketing, required purchases and the original fee. That can be excellent value if the brand brings customers and buying power you could not get alone — but it should be a conscious decision. Run your own numbers in the royalty cost calculator.

Questions to ask about costs

  • What did your last ten franchisees actually spend to open?
  • Which fees have increased in the last five years, and by how much?
  • Does the franchisor receive rebates from required suppliers, and how are they used?
  • Can I see the last two years of ad fund financial statements?
  • When will the next mandatory remodel or technology upgrade happen, and what will it cost?
