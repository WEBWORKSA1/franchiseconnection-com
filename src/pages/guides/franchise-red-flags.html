No franchise is risk-free, and a single red flag is not automatically a deal-breaker. But each one below deserves a clear written explanation from the franchisor, and several together should make you walk away.

In the sales process

  1. Pressure to sign quickly. "Only two territories left", "price goes up next week". A good system qualifies you as carefully as you evaluate it.
  2. Earnings promises outside Item 19. If a salesperson tells you what you will earn but the FDD has no financial performance representation — or the figure differs — that is a serious compliance problem.
  3. Discouraging validation calls or steering you only to hand-picked franchisees.
  4. Vague answers about support, or a sales team that cannot introduce you to the people who would support you after opening.
  5. Asking for money before the 14-day period ends. The FTC Rule requires the FDD at least 14 calendar days before any payment or signature.

In the FDD

  1. High or rising turnover in Item 20. Many terminations, non-renewals, ceased operations or transfers relative to the size of the system.
  2. Units sold but not opened. A large backlog of signed-but-unopened units can mean the franchisor sells faster than it can support.
  3. Weak financial statements in Item 21. Negative equity, going-concern notes, or reliance on new franchise fees rather than royalties.
  4. Litigation patterns in Item 3. Repeated franchisee claims about the same issue.
  5. Leadership churn in Item 2. Frequent changes of CEO, COO or development leadership.
  6. No protected territory in Item 12, or broad rights for the franchisor to sell into your area through other channels.
  7. Heavy supplier markups or large rebates to the franchisor in Item 8 without clear benefits to franchisees.
  8. Unregistered trademarks in Item 13.
  9. One-sided Item 17 terms: short cure periods, broad termination rights, steep transfer conditions or long non-competes.

In the franchisee network

  1. Owners won't talk, or many have signed agreements that limit what they can say.
  2. Consistent complaints about the same problem from different owners and regions.
  3. Most owners would not buy again. Ask every one of them.
  4. Reported investment far above Item 7's high estimate.

What to do when you see one

Write the issue down, ask the franchisor for an explanation in writing, and raise it with your franchise attorney. Some flags have innocent explanations: a wave of transfers can reflect a large multi-unit owner selling to another; a lawsuit can be a single landlord dispute. Others do not. The test is whether the explanation is consistent with the documents and with what franchisees tell you.

If you believe a franchise seller has misled you in the US, you can report it to the Federal Trade Commission and your state's franchise regulator. In Canada, provinces with franchise legislation give franchisees statutory rights where disclosure is deficient.
