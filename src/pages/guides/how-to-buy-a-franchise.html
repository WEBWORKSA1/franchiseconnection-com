Buying a franchise usually takes three to nine months from first research to signing, and another three to twelve months to open. Most of the risk is concentrated in a handful of decisions: how much you commit, which category you choose, and whether you verify the franchisor's claims before you sign. The ten steps below put those decisions in the right order.

1. Decide whether franchising suits you

A franchise trades independence for a tested system. You follow the franchisor's operating manual, buy from approved suppliers, use their brand standards and pay ongoing royalties. In return you get a recognisable brand, training, purchasing power and a playbook that has already been debugged in other markets.

People who thrive as franchisees tend to enjoy executing a proven process, managing people, and selling locally. People who struggle usually want to reinvent the product or resent being told how to run "their" business. Be honest about which describes you. Ask yourself:

  • Am I comfortable following someone else's system even when I would do it differently?
  • Do I want to work in the business daily, or manage a manager?
  • Can my household go 12 to 24 months without the business paying me a full salary?

2. Know your numbers before you shop

Franchisors qualify buyers on two figures: liquid capital (cash and investments you can access quickly) and net worth. Lenders add a third: credit history. Work these out before talking to any brand, because the conversation changes once a salesperson knows you have more to spend.

A practical rule: lenders financing a new franchise commonly ask you to inject 20–30% of the total project cost in cash. If you have $120,000 of liquid capital and want to keep six months of living expenses in reserve, your realistic project size is roughly $300,000 to $400,000, not the $120,000 you have in the bank. Our affordability check does this math for you.

3. Shortlist categories, then brands

Start with categories rather than logos. A home-services franchise and a quick-service restaurant have completely different economics, staffing, hours and capital needs. Choose two or three categories that match your budget, the role you want and your background, then look for brands within them.

The category explorer shows typical investment, fees, owner role and the main risk for 75 categories. Use it to rule things out quickly. Then build a list of four to eight brands per category from franchisor websites, franchise expos, association directories and advisors.

4. Request information and have the first calls

Your first conversation with a franchisor's development team is a two-way screen. They are checking your capital, motivation and fit. You are checking whether they answer straight questions directly. Good early questions include: how many units opened and closed last year, what the average time to open is, what support looks like in the first 90 days, and whether territory is available where you want to operate.

5. Read the Franchise Disclosure Document

In the United States, the FTC Franchise Rule requires the franchisor to give you its Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) at least 14 calendar days before you sign a binding agreement or pay any money. The FDD has 23 required items. Four deserve the most attention:

  • Item 7, the estimated initial investment, line by line.
  • Item 19, financial performance representations — the only place a franchisor may state what units earn. Not every franchisor provides one.
  • Item 20, outlets and franchisee information, including openings, transfers, terminations and closures, plus contact details for current and recently departed franchisees.
  • Item 21, the franchisor's audited financial statements.

Our guide to all 23 items explains what to look for in each.

6. Validate with franchisees

Validation calls are where you learn what the FDD cannot tell you. Call at least ten franchisees across different tenures and regions, and include former owners listed in Item 20. Ask what they actually invested, how long it took to break even, how many hours they work, what the franchisor does well, and whether they would buy again. A strong system produces consistent answers. Use our 50 validation questions.

7. Build a business plan and line up financing

Turn what you learned into a monthly projection for the first three years: revenue ramp, cost of goods, labor, rent, royalties, marketing and debt payments. Lenders will want this plan along with your personal financial statement and résumé.

Common sources are SBA 7(a) loans, SBA 504 loans for real estate and heavy equipment, rollovers as business start-ups (ROBS) using retirement funds, home-equity lines, equipment leasing and franchisor financing programs. Each has different costs and risks; see the financing guide. In the US, lenders check the SBA Franchise Directory to confirm a brand is eligible for SBA-guaranteed loans.

8. Attend discovery day

Discovery day is a visit — in person or virtual — to meet the leadership team, see training and operations, and for both sides to decide. Treat it as an interview of the franchisor. Meet the people who will support you after opening, not just the sales team, and ask to see the technology you will use every day.

9. Get professional review, then sign

Hire a franchise attorney to review the FDD and franchise agreement, and an accountant to review your projections and the franchisor's financial statements. Some terms are negotiable, especially for multi-unit deals or in new markets: development schedules, territory boundaries, opening deadlines and occasionally fee timing. If the franchisor fills in or materially changes the agreement after giving you the FDD, the FTC Rule requires that you receive the completed agreement at least 7 calendar days before signing.

10. Open, then run the playbook

After signing come site selection and lease negotiation (if you need premises), build-out, hiring, training and pre-opening marketing. Track your progress against the opening timeline in Item 11. In the first year, follow the system closely, attend every training, and measure the handful of numbers that predict success in your category — usually customer count, average ticket, labor percentage and recurring revenue.
