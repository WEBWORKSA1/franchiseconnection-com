Franchising is a growth strategy and a regulated sale of a business opportunity at the same time. You license your brand and system to independent owners, who pay you an initial fee and ongoing royalties. Done well, it can expand a brand far faster than company-owned growth. Done early or carelessly, it can multiply your problems across dozens of locations and expose you to legal claims.

Is your business franchisable?

Before spending on lawyers, test your business honestly against these questions:

  • Proven profitability. Has at least one location — ideally two or more — been consistently profitable for a couple of years?
  • Replicability. Can someone without your personal skills run it by following documented procedures?
  • Transferable demand. Will customers in other cities want it, or does it depend on your local reputation?
  • Margin for a royalty. After paying a 5–8% royalty and fund contributions, will a franchisee still earn an attractive return on their investment?
  • Teachability. Can the system be learned in weeks, not years?
  • Distinctiveness. Is there a brand, method or product worth paying for?

If the honest answer to the margin question is no, consider alternatives such as licensing, company-owned expansion or partnerships.

The franchise development steps

  1. Strategy and unit economics. Model what a franchisee invests, earns and pays you. Set the franchise fee, royalty, ad fund, territory size and development schedule based on that model, not on competitors' numbers.
  2. Protect the brand. Register your trademarks. A franchise system built on an unregistered or contested mark is fragile.
  3. Form the franchisor entity. Most franchisors create a separate company to sign franchise agreements and hold the franchise program.
  4. Prepare the FDD and franchise agreement with an experienced franchise attorney. The FDD's 23 items include audited financial statements of the franchisor entity.
  5. Register where required. Several US states require franchisors to register or file their FDD before offering franchises there. Canadian provinces with franchise statutes require disclosure documents in their own formats.
  6. Write the operations manual covering every repeatable task, from opening checklists to hiring, customer service, marketing and reporting.
  7. Build training. Classroom and in-store training, onboarding timelines and certification for owners and managers.
  8. Set up support. Field support, technology, approved supplier agreements and a marketing fund with clear governance.
  9. Launch franchise sales and marketing. A franchise-development website, lead generation, a qualification process, discovery days and a compliant sales script.
  10. Award carefully. Your first franchisees define your reputation. Turn away buyers who are under-capitalised or a poor fit.

What it costs to franchise

Costs vary widely with the number of states, the complexity of your system and whether you use a full-service development firm. Budget for legal work on the FDD and agreements, an audit of the franchisor entity, trademark filings, state registration fees, the operations manual and training materials, and — the line most owners underestimate — the marketing and staff time needed to sell and support the first franchisees. Many emerging franchisors spend as much in the first two years on franchise sales as on legal setup.

The compliance basics

  • Give each prospect your FDD at least 14 calendar days before they sign or pay, and the completed agreement at least 7 calendar days before signing if you change material terms.
  • Only make earnings claims that appear in Item 19, with a reasonable basis and written substantiation.
  • Update the FDD annually and when material changes occur.
  • Keep sales staff and brokers trained on what they may and may not say.

Selling your first franchises

New franchisors typically combine their own website and local network with franchise portals, consultant networks (which charge a placement commission) and franchise expos. Track cost per qualified lead and cost per award, and accept that the first awards take longest. Signs you are not ready yet

  • Your only location depends on you being there every day.
  • Profit comes mainly from your unpaid labor.
  • You have not documented how anything is done.
  • You cannot fund two years of franchise development before royalties cover the cost.
